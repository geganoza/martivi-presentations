Every number in this report is a share of something. Get the something wrong and every scenario is wrong by the same factor, so this part establishes it first and states plainly how solid it is.

Georgia counts households only at a census. The last published household count is the 2014 census. Population is estimated every year, households are not. That single fact governs how much confidence any Tbilisi market size deserves.

The series has a break in it, and anyone presenting from it must say so

Between 1 January 2024 and 1 January 2025 the national figure moves from 3,694.6 to 3,930.4 thousand — +235.8 thousand, +6.4% — and Tbilisi from 1,258.5 to 1,335.7 thousand (+6.1%). Georgia did not gain a quarter of a million people in a year. This is the 2024 General Population Census rebasing the estimate. Figures for 2024 and earlier sit on the 2014 census base; 2025 and later sit on the 2024 base. They are not a like-for-like series, and a chart that draws a straight line through the join is wrong.

The consequence for this study is direct: every market size produced before this census — including the one in the business plan under review — was built on a population roughly 6% too low.

How many households are there in Tbilisi?

Three answers, and the honest one is that only the first is a count.

Which of these the report actually uses

411,098 — the 2024 census count. Every scenario in Part 7 divides into that figure and no other. The carry-forward row is shown only to demonstrate how far an estimate drifts from a count in two years, and the 2014 row only for the decade comparison.

What the census does not publish is a breakdown of Tbilisi dwellings by construction year — the Living Conditions release runs to table 7 plus table 12, with no year-of-completion table. That is why the vintage split later in this part has to be carried forward from 2014.

Two things in that table change how the product should be built

86.9% of Tbilisi households live in an apartment. An individual gas boiler in an apartment is a wall-hung combi on an outside wall with a flue through it — exactly the product the customs record in Part 3 shows arriving, and a standardised, repeatable service job.

14.2% rent, up from 8.6% in 2014, and 39.1% of Tbilisi households are one or two people. A tenant does not buy maintenance cover on someone else's boiler, and a flat monthly fee is a much heavier burden per person on a one-person household than the average suggests. Both cut directly into the addressable share used in Part 7.

What kind of dwellings they are

This matters more than it looks. An individual gas boiler in an apartment is a wall-hung combi on an outside wall with a flue through it; in a house it can be anything. Tbilisi is overwhelmingly apartments, which is exactly the product the import data in Part 3 shows arriving.

Can a Tbilisi household actually pay every month?

Read these as household rates, not adult rates

Findex measures adults: 64.5% own a debit card, 78.8% hold an account. A subscription needs one banked member per household, not every member banked. Converting on the census composition — 16.9% of Tbilisi households are one person, average size 3.23 — gives 85–88% of households by card and 94–95% by account, and a standing order runs off an account. Geostat's own household-level measure, 96.9% with internet access, points the same way.

Part 7 therefore treats 85–96% of boiler households as able to pay. It is a capability test only: whether they want the product is the penetration rate, which is 2% to 12.2%.

How old the housing is — and how fast that is changing

This is the table that explains the single most contested number in the report, and it needs carrying forward, because the only Tbilisi breakdown by construction year is the 2014 census. The 2024 census counted households and dwellings but has not published a year-of-completion table — its Living Conditions release runs to table 7 plus table 12, and there is no construction-period table in it. I checked the full file list.

So the vintage split below is the 2014 census carried forward on the household growth between the two censuses. Both endpoints are counts, and the only assumption is that households added between 2014 and 2024 are living in stock built in that period — which for a city completing 1.6 million m² a year is close enough to state.

The number that matters is the last one, and it moves fast

At the 2014 census, 5.9% of Tbilisi dwellings had been built after 2000. Ten years later that share is 23.8% — 97,313 dwellings, four times as many. Tbilisi added 77,725 occupied households between the two censuses, essentially all of it new build.

Now put that beside Part 2: 25.6% of urban households have a gas central heating system, and 23.8% of Tbilisi dwellings were built after 2000. Those two numbers are almost the same, and that is not a coincidence — a dwelling completed after 2000 is handed over with an individual boiler and a radiator circuit as standard, because there is no district heating to connect to and the building was designed for it.

The rest of the boiler base is the older stock that has been renovated. Part 2 puts that at 42% of urban dwellings reconstructed since 2001, with the ratio of the two an upper bound on the renovation heating. New build and renovation are the two doors into this market, and both are measurable.

Two thirds of the city is still Soviet-built, and that is the ceiling on the near term

Even after the biggest construction decade in the city's history, 66.7% of Tbilisi dwellings still predate 1991 — down from 82.4%, but still two in three. Those flats were built for district heating or a gas convector. They have no radiator circuit, and roughly half of them are concrete panel (Part 2, Table 3.5), which is the hardest construction to retrofit pipe into.

That is the structural ceiling on how fast a boiler-maintenance market can grow in Tbilisi, and it is why the growth rate in Part 2 is 8.4% a year rather than the 33% a year the plan under review assumes. The housing stock cannot convert faster than it renovates.

New supply — the flow of new boiler installations

There is a reporting break in 2018 and it is enormous

Tbilisi residential completions go from 37 buildings and 49,748 m² in 2017 to 613 buildings and 1,008,299 m² in 2018. A city does not multiply its construction output twentyfold in a year. This is a change in what gets declared and counted, not in what got built.

Consequence: do not build a trend through 2018, and treat 2015–2017 as under-reported rather than as a slump. The census-to-census figure above — 77,725 more occupied households — is unaffected by this break, which is why the vintage table is built on censuses and not on this series.

Gross completions are not new customers

Tbilisi completes roughly 1.6 million m² of residential floor area a year, which on the census average apartment size implies 13,500–18,000 new dwellings a year. That is the gross flow of new boiler installations and it is a real, large number.

But net growth in occupied apartments between the 2014 and 2024 censuses was only about 7,830 a year — roughly half the gross completion rate. The difference is empty units, second homes and short-let investment stock. A plan that sizes new installations off completions and then assumes each becomes a paying household is counting the same wedge twice.

Heating

Read this table exactly as it is printed

The four rows are mutually exclusive and sum to 100%. A household is in one of them. So 25.6% of urban households have a gas central heating system, and 72.9% have individual heating facilities — which is Geostat's term for gas convectors, wood stoves and electric heaters. Those are not boilers and they are not in the service catalogue.

Combining the two tables: 72.9% of urban households heat with individual facilities, and 59.4% of those use natural gas. That is roughly 43% of urban households heating with gas convectors — a large, gas-connected, non-boiler population. They are a market for something, but not for boiler maintenance.

An independent check on the 25.6%, from the same survey

Table 3.10 asks a different question — how much floor area do you actually heat — and gets an answer that corroborates Table 3.11. Only 33.5% of urban households heat 51 sq.m or more (29.3% + 4.2%). Whole-dwelling heating and a whole-dwelling heating system should be close, and 33.5% against 25.6% is close. Two questions, two tables, consistent answers.

This matters because Part 3 will show a customs record that is very hard to reconcile with 25.6%. Before reaching for the conclusion that the survey undercounts, note that the survey agrees with itself.

The trend — the only two observations that exist

This is the single most important table in Part 2, and also the most attackable

Urban gas central heating went from 17.1% to 25.6% in five years — a measured growth rate of 8.4% a year, from two independent samples five years apart. It is the only trend evidence in existence, and it is what Part 7 uses to carry the 2022 measurement forward to the present.

Now the caveats, stated before anyone else states them. These are two separate cross-sections, not a panel. The 2017 publication states its sampling error as 2.1% at country level and “no more than 5%” at urban/rural level, and does not say whether that means five percentage points or five per cent relative — on the first reading an 8.5 pp move is only marginally outside it. The 2022 publication states no margin of error at all. And the 2017 table counts dwellings while the 2022 table counts households.

The national move (10.5 → 17.1) is comfortably outside the stated national error and is not seriously contestable. The urban move is directionally certain and quantitatively soft. Use it as a range, never as a point.

Why the number is what it is — the building stock

A quarter of urban households on a gas central heating system reads like a statement about money. It is at least as much a statement about pipes.

A central heating system is not an appliance you buy — it is a boiler plus a circuit: flow and return pipe routed to a radiator in every room. In a flat built for district heating or for a gas convector, that circuit does not exist and there is nowhere in the structure to put it. Installing one means lifting floors or running pipe along skirtings through every room. That is a renovation, and it is priced and decided like a renovation.

The same Geostat survey that reports the heating tables also reports when the dwelling was built and when it was last reconstructed. Put those beside each other and the 25.6% stops looking like weak demand.

The constraint is physical, and it is measurable

Three quarters of the urban housing stock — 76.0% of all urban dwellings, 91.1% of those that stated a year — predates 1991. Almost half of urban dwellings are concrete panel (48.9%, Table 3.5), the hardest construction of all to retrofit pipe into.

Against that, 42.0% of urban dwellings have been reconstructed since 2001 and 34.9% never at all. So the population that can physically host a central heating system without major work is roughly the renovated two fifths, not the whole stock.

Set the two side by side — 25.6% on gas central heating against 42.0% renovated — and the 25.6% stops looking like weak demand and starts looking like a limit on the supply of suitable dwellings. The ratio is 61.0%, but Geostat publishes no cross-tabulation between the heating table and the reconstruction table, so that is an upper bound on the renovation channel, not a measured conversion rate — and some boilers arrive in new build, which Table 3.4 records as not reconstructed at all.

What this changes for the business

  • The addressable base grows with renovation and new build, not with income. Roughly 3.58% of the urban stock renovates each year, and Tbilisi added 77,725 occupied households between the 2014 and 2024 censuses — almost all of it new build. Those two flows, not advertising, are what create boiler households. Part 1 carries the Tbilisi vintage split forward: the post-2000 share of the housing stock has gone from 5.9% to 23.8% in a decade, which is within two points of the 25.6% on gas central heating.
  • The un-renovated majority is not a dormant market — it is a different market. A flat with a gas convector and a kolonka has two gas appliances that need servicing and no radiator circuit. It will not buy a boiler-maintenance subscription because it has no boiler.
  • The installed base skews new, and that cuts against the repair business. If boilers arrive with renovations and new build, the average boiler in Tbilisi is younger than the housing around it. A young boiler breaks less often — and while it is inside its guarantee the Georgian importer repairs it free (Gorgia advertises exactly that). So a young base means fewer paid repair jobs, not more. Whether the manufacturer guarantee also obliges the household to buy an annual service is unverified in Georgia; it is a hard condition in the UK and it should not be assumed here. See Part 6.
  • Insulation is almost absent. Only 4.8% of urban dwellings have insulated external walls (Table 3.6), against 55.3% with insulated windows. A boiler in an uninsulated concrete flat runs harder and longer for the same comfort — more hours, more cycles, more service.

Hot water

The 97.2% does not mean 97.2% of households own a boiler

This is the single most misread number in the whole dataset, and it was misread twice in the earlier stages of this engagement before being withdrawn both times.

Geostat's own definition, in the methodology section at the back of the publication, is that a water heating system is central when the dwelling is connected to a municipal network, and individual when the household heats water with its own facilities — a water heating boiler, a gas stove, an electric oven or a wood stove. Georgia has almost no municipal hot water, so nearly everyone is 'individual' by definition.

Two consequences. First, the 97.2% contains the 25.6%: a combi boiler is simultaneously the Table 3.11 central heating system and the Table 3.18 water heater. Multiplying the two double-counts. Second, subtracting them (97.2 − 25.6 = 71.6) produces a number that appears nowhere in the publication and is a ceiling, not a measurement. Both operations were performed earlier in this engagement and both are formally withdrawn.

The small residual — the 2.8% of urban households with no dedicated water-heating appliance at all — heat water on the gas stove (2.7%) or a wood stove (0.9%). That is Table 3.20 of the same publication, and it is the only table in this section with nothing in it for a service business: those households have no appliance to service. Its shares are of all households and overlap with the rows above, which is a standing trap for anyone quoting it.

Air conditioning

Air conditioning is the second-largest system in the service catalogue and its household base is much smaller than the boiler base: 81.6% of urban households have none at all.

What this publication does not contain

Three absences, each checked page by page

  1. No Tbilisi split. The publication reports Total / Urban / Rural and nothing finer. Every Tbilisi figure in this report is therefore an extrapolation from urban Georgia, and is labelled as one. Tbilisi is roughly 55% of the urban population, so urban Georgia is dominated by, but not identical to, Tbilisi.
  2. No cross-tabulation between the heating table and the water-heating table. How the 25.6% and the 97.2% intersect is not published, which is precisely why the arithmetic in the box above cannot be repaired — the joint distribution does not exist in the public data.
  3. No boiler count. The survey counts households by system type, not appliances. A household with two boilers counts once.
  4. And the frame underneath it is the 2014 census. The Survey Programme (printed p.9) states the sampling frame, and Table 4.2 lets you recover the weighting universe: about 1,109,200 households — which is the 2014 census count, not a 2022 one. The 2024 census counted 1,201,564. So roughly 92,000 households, 7.7% of the country, sat outside the frame the 2022 survey grossed up to.

That last one matters more than it sounds, and it points in one direction. A frame built in 2014 under-covers everything built since — and what was built since is overwhelmingly new apartments, in Tbilisi, which are precisely the dwellings handed over with a boiler and a radiator circuit as standard. Tbilisi added 71,794 households between the two censuses on the all-households basis (411,098 against 339,304). between the two censuses.

A rate measured on a 2014 frame therefore probably understates boiler penetration today, and the bias runs one way. How much is not quantifiable from published data, so nothing in this report adjusts for it — but it is the fourth independent reason to treat 25.6% as a floor rather than a central estimate, and it is the one a statistician in the room will recognise fastest.

This is the hardest evidence in the report and it is the part that contradicts Part 2. Customs declarations are not a survey: every line is a shipment that a company paid duty on. Two independent extracts are used — the official Geostat value series, which runs long but stops at 2019, and transaction-level declaration lines for 2023 and 2024 supplied by Termorum, which carry quantities as well as values.

Getting the commodity code right is the whole game here, so it is stated explicitly before any number: 8403.10 is central heating boilers. Instantaneous gas water heaters — the Georgian კოლონკა — are 8419.11, a different heading entirely, and electric storage heaters of the Thermex type are 8516.10. They are counted separately below, so the boiler figures are boilers.

Units, four years

Two totals circulate for 2024 and they are both defensible

Termorum publish a curated 2024 total of 74,565 units. Summing every import declaration line on HS 8403.10 in the same file gives 77,170. The 2,605-unit difference sits almost entirely in China (2,080) and Italy (464) and is an exclusion Termorum applied without documenting it in the file — plausibly shipments they judged not to be domestic wall-hung boilers.

This report uses the declaration-line total and shows the curated one beside it. The difference is 3.4% and changes no conclusion, but presenting one figure while a partner circulates another is how a meeting goes wrong.

Where they come from

Three origins are 96.5% of the market and they are three different products at three different prices: Turkey at $421 and China at $267 are volume, Italy at $437 is the brand tier. That spread is the single best available proxy for what the installed base is worth to service — a $267 boiler and a $437 boiler are not the same maintenance customer.

Re-export, seasonality, and what kind of boiler

Re-export does not explain the gap

2,970 units left the country in 2024 against 77,170 that came in — 3.8%, all of it to Azerbaijan and Armenia. Net supply into Georgia was 74,200 units. Whatever explains the difference between the customs record and the household survey, it is not that these boilers are passing through.

This settles a question that would otherwise sit open: the import stream is almost entirely domestic-scale wall-hung boilers. Floor-standing units — the large commercial machines — are 34 of 71,033 in 2023, at an average $7,913 against $402 for wall-hung. So the volume cannot be waved away as industrial plant, and it cannot be waved away as re-export. Whatever it is, it is domestic-scale equipment being installed in Georgia.

The long series, and the repair market at the border

Two things this table proves

The boiler is the main event, not the kolonka. In 2019 Georgia imported $18.71M of central heating boilers against $5.78M of instantaneous gas water heaters. Whatever share of Georgian households own a kolonka, the money and the engineering are in boilers, and they are separate commodity codes so there is no chance the boiler figure is contaminated by water heaters.

The parts market is small and it is measurable. Boiler parts ran at 4.3–6.9% of boiler value every year for eleven years — $7.26M cumulative over 2010–19 against $137.45M of boilers. This is the repair economy measured at the customs border, independent of anybody's survey, and it is the closest thing that exists to an external check on how much boiler repair actually happens in Georgia. It is not a large number.

What the flow implies about the installed base

The discrepancy, on the right denominators

25.6% is urban Georgia, not Tbilisi — Geostat publishes no Tbilisi split. On the 2024 census that rate means:

  • 193,000 urban households nationally on a gas central heating system (754,343 urban households × 25.6%)
  • 105,000 in Tbilisi, if Tbilisi sits on the urban-Georgia average (411,098 × 25.6%)
  • 205,000 nationally on Geostat's all-Georgia rate of 17.1%

Against that, customs records 68,000–79,000 boilers a year entering the country, almost all wall-hung and domestic, with re-export under 4%. The next section establishes how far those two can be reconciled.

Two limits on the survey figure before it is used. Tbilisi is roughly half of urban Georgia, so 25.6% is blended with Kutaisi, Batumi, Rustavi and every other town, and nothing published says whether Tbilisi sits above or below it. And the 2022 survey grosses up to the 2014 census — a weighting universe of about 1,109,200 households against the 1,201,564 the 2024 census counted, with the 92,000 outside that frame being disproportionately the new Tbilisi apartments that come with a boiler fitted. That second point has a known direction: it makes 25.6% more likely to be a floor than a ceiling.

Reconciling the two records

Two independent measurements disagree, so the reconciliation has to do three things: build the boiler stock from the actual import history rather than a single year, check the method against cases where the answer is already published, and only then say what the discrepancy means.

The import series used here is UN Comtrade, which reproduces the partner-supplied declaration totals exactly for 2021 (67,097 units) and 2022 (59,124) and carries quantities as well as values. That agreement across three independent sources — Comtrade, Geostat and the customs declarations held by Termorum — is what makes the unit counts safe to build on.

One point of method matters. Boilers and kolonkas are separate commodity codes, which is why the survey's water-heating tables can be brought into this at all: the reconciliation does not require the codes to overlap, only that boilers and kolonkas together supply the water-heating stock the survey measures.

The reconciliation that works, in four lines

  1. Geostat Table 3.18: 93.0% of Georgian households have an individual water-heating system.
  2. Geostat Table 3.19: 77.7% of them use natural gas for it. On the census count of 1,201,564 households that is 868,262 households heating water with gas.
  3. Customs: the gas appliances that do that job arrived in 2024 as 79,054.0 boilers and 101,023.0 kolonkas — boilers are 43.9% of the flow.
  4. If that split holds in the stock, 381,168 households — 31.7% of all households — have a gas boiler.

Against Geostat's heating table, which says 17.1%. The two are no longer three or four times apart; they are about twice apart, and the residual has a plain explanation: a household can own a wall-hung gas boiler and still not describe its heating as a “central system.” Geostat's Table 3.10 shows 33.9% of urban households heating only 16–30 m² — a gas appliance warming one or two rooms and making hot water. In Table 3.11 that household answers “individual heating facilities”. In Table 3.18 the same household answers “individual water heater”. It owns a boiler either way, and it needs the boiler serviced either way.

The test that needs no assumption about Tbilisi

Everything above depends on splitting the country into Tbilisi and the rest, which the survey does not publish. This one does not. It asks a single question: can a stock of 205,467 boilers — which is what the survey's 17.1% means nationally — absorb 79,054 boilers a year?

A stock consumes new units for two reasons: replacing units that die, and growing. The replacement rate is the stock divided by the boiler's service life. The growth rate is published — the survey itself measured 10.5% to 17.1% over five years, which on the census household count is about 15,900 additional households a year.

How long a Georgian boiler actually lasts — the first local anchor on this number

Everything in this section turns on service life, and until now that parameter had no Georgian source at all: the 8-to-15-year range used earlier was European practice carried across.

Tbilisi boiler service professionals report 8–10 years in the city and 6–7 years in the rest of the country. Weighted by household share — Tbilisi is 34.2% of Georgian households — that is about 7.4 years nationally. Shorter life in the regions is what you would expect: harder water, less regular servicing, cheaper units.

This is trade testimony, not a published statistic, and it comes from a small number of practitioners. It should be widened by asking several service centres independently. But it is the only Georgian evidence on the point that exists, and it is better than an imported assumption.

It moves the answer, and it moves it toward the survey. At 12 years the import flow implied a national stock of 63.1% of households — implausible on its face. At 7.4 years it implies 38.9%, which sits between the survey's water-heating reading (32%) and its trend reading (35.4% urban). Three methods that were far apart are now close.

What it does not do is close the absorption gap. Even at a six-year life the survey's stock needs 50,105 boilers a year and 79,054 arrive — a surplus of nearly 29,000 units annually. Shortening the life raises the replacement requirement and still leaves a large unexplained inflow.

And it makes the service market bigger, not smaller

A shorter service life cuts two ways and the second way is the commercially important one. If the average Georgian boiler lasts 7.4 years rather than twelve, then at any moment a larger share of the installed base is in the second half of its life, where faults cluster and parts start failing.

It also means the replacement market is large and continuous: roughly 34,245 to 25,683 units a year nationally are replacing something that has died, before any growth. For a service business that is a steady flow of end-of-life diagnosis, emergency call-outs in winter, and replacement quotations — the highest-value work in the trade.

The fault-incidence range used in Part 7 (0.20 to 0.40 a year) is anchored to UK claims data on a younger, better-maintained stock. A shorter-lived Georgian base argues for the top of that range rather than the middle, and that is worth testing against Casa Calda's own service records.

The size of the surplus

At any credible boiler life, the survey's national stock needs roughly 30,000–36,000 boilers a year. 79,054 arrived in 2024, and under 4% of them left again. Run it the other way and the flow implies a national stock of 630,000 to 760,000 — 53% to 63% of Georgian households — against the survey's 17.1%.

That is a large discrepancy. Before drawing a conclusion from it, the method that produced it has to be tested, because it can be tested.

Testing the method on products where the answer is already known

The absorption test is only as good as the flow-to-stock method behind it. Geostat also measures the water-heating appliance stock, and customs also records water-heating appliance imports — so the same method can be run on products whose answer is published. If it fails there, it cannot be trusted on the boiler.

What the control test establishes

  • Kolonka: 0.93×. The method almost exactly reproduces Geostat's published figure — 808,000 implied against 868,000 measured. On an unambiguous product with an unambiguous survey question, flow-to-stock works.
  • Electric water heaters: 4.29×. It fails badly. But the reason is visible in the price: the average unit on this commodity code cost $15 in 2024, down from $51 in 2019. At $15 this is not a Thermex tank, it is immersion elements and kettle-class goods. The code is contaminated with things that are not household water heaters, so the numerator is counting the wrong objects.
  • Boilers: 2.59×. Here neither explanation applies. The code was checked directly (Part 3): 8403.10 is central heating boilers, kolonkas sit under 8419.11, and the 2023 declarations show floor-standing commercial units are 0.05% of the volume. At $383 a unit these are real domestic appliances, not cheap goods. So the boiler discrepancy is not the same artefact that explains the electric one.

Is the recent inflow a bubble?

Everything above rests on 2024 arrivals. If that year is inflated — by COVID-era home improvement, or by the population influx after February 2022 — then the bridge is built on a run-rate that will not last. It is a reasonable worry and it is testable.

The answer is no, and the data says the opposite of what you would expect

  • Growth was faster before 2020 than after. Units grew +13.6% a year over 2015–19 and +7.8% a year over 2019–24. The long-run customs value series runs at +12.2% a year from 2009.
  • 2024 sits below the pre-COVID trend, not above it. Had 2015–19 growth simply continued, 2024 would have been 103,005 units. It was 79,054 — -23% below.
  • 2022, the year of the largest migration influx, saw imports fall 12%. If new arrivals were driving a boiler boom it does not show up in the year they arrived.

This is a fifteen-year secular trend that has been decelerating, not a post-2020 spike. Using 2024 as the basis for the bridge is therefore conservative rather than generous.

The mix has just shifted hard, and it changes what the installed base is made of

Chinese units went from 4.5% of the market in 2022 to 31.4% in 2024, at $267 against $421 for Turkish and $437 for Italian. That single shift is why the 2024 average CIF fell to $379 from $406 the year before, and it is a recent change — before it, the market was predominantly Turkish and Italian at a higher price.

Two consequences follow, and the second one matters commercially.

For the back-cast: if the pre-2015 mix was European and Turkish, the unit price then was higher than the $394 used to convert those years' customs values into units — so the early unit counts above are, if anything, overstated. At $430 the 2009 figure falls from about 15,000 to 13,700, and at $460 to 12,800. That makes the pre-2015 base smaller still, which reinforces rather than weakens the point above: at a 7.4-year life those vintages are gone either way.

For the service business: a $267 boiler does not last as long as a $437 one. The trade's reported 6–10 year life is consistent with a market that has been buying at the cheap end, and the 2024 shift suggests the average life is falling, not stable. That points to a rising failure rate and a shortening replacement cycle over the next five years — which is bad for the household and good for whoever services it. It is also the most direct thing Casa Calda could verify from its own job records: failures by brand and by age.

The corollary — the installed base is young, and that is why the starting year matters less than it looks

Imports in 2009 were roughly a fifth of today's. That would matter a great deal if boilers lasted fifteen years, because the base would carry a long tail of old units. At the 7.4-year life the Georgian trade reports, it barely matters at all: only the 2019–2024 vintages are still in service, and everything imported before that has already been replaced — which is itself part of what the replacement line in the bridge is counting.

Those surviving vintages leave a stock of about 373,266 boilers net of re-export. That is the number the bridge has to distribute between households, commercial premises and empty new-build, and it is why the earlier caveat about pre-2015 data being unavailable turned out not to bind.

Where the boilers actually go

The surplus is only a mystery if every boiler is assumed to land in an occupied household. Once the service life is the one the Georgian trade reports, and commercial premises and unoccupied new dwellings are counted, most of it has somewhere to go.

The gap that dominated this study mostly closes

Earlier drafts described a discrepancy of three to four times that nobody had reconciled. On the arithmetic above it is 10.4% to 23.0% of the annual inflow — between 7,920 and 17,467 boilers a year.

Three corrections did the work, and none of them was available at the start:

  1. Service life. A European 12–15 years implied a national stock of 63% of households, which was never credible. The Georgian trade reports 7.4 years, which raises the replacement requirement by half and absorbs 27,766 units a year on the survey's own stock.
  2. Commercial premises. Part 4 sizes these at 33,000–55,000 nationally. They consume 5,797–9,662 replacement boilers a year and no household survey counts one of them.
  3. Dwellings completed but not occupied. A new flat is handed over with a boiler on the wall. Tbilisi completes 13,500–18,000 dwellings a year against 7,830 of net occupied growth, and Tbilisi is 79% of national completions — so 7,159–12,841 boilers a year are installed where no survey can find a household.

What the residual is worth, if it is households

If the remaining 7,920–17,467 units a year are household boilers that the survey did not classify as a central heating system — the mechanism Part 2 describes, where a boiler heating two rooms is reported as "individual heating facilities" — then at a 7.4-year life they represent 58,611 to 129,256 additional boiler households.

That takes national household penetration from the survey's 17.1% to 22.0%–27.9%. Urban penetration would be proportionally higher, which is consistent with the survey's own urban figure of 25.6% in May 2022 and its own trend of 8.4% a year.

So the two records are no longer in conflict. The survey undercounts household boilers by roughly five to eleven points, for a reason Part 2 explains, and the rest of the import flow goes to commercial premises and empty new-build. Nothing here requires either source to be wrong about what it measured.

The residual is a balancing figure, not a measurement. It carries every error in the five lines above it, and the two widest — commercial premises and the unoccupied wedge — are themselves derived. Treat 22%–28% as the range the evidence supports, not as a point estimate.

What the discrepancy means, and what it does not

The control test sets the limit on how hard this evidence can be pushed. A method that reproduces one published stock to within 7% and misses another by more than four times cannot by itself prove what the boiler surplus consists of. What it establishes is that the surplus is real and is not a coding artefact.

There are two explanations, and the published data does not separate them:

  1. Boilers fitted into dwellings nobody lives in yet. A new apartment is handed over with a boiler on the wall whether or not anyone moves in; a kolonka is bought by an occupant. That asymmetry is specific to boilers and is exactly what the kolonka control cannot detect. Part 1 measures the wedge in Tbilisi: gross completions imply 92,000–123,000 dwellings over 2015–2025 against 77,725 additional occupied households — that figure being on the houses-and-apartments basis the vintage table uses (409,504 against 331,779), not the all-households basis.
  2. Households that own a boiler and do not call their heating a central system. A boiler in an un-renovated flat heats two rooms and makes hot water; its owner answers "individual heating facilities". Part 2 gives this a mechanism — three quarters of urban dwellings predate 1991 and have no radiator circuit — and Table 3.10 is consistent with it, showing a third of urban households heating only 16–30 m².

Both point the same way: the household boiler base is larger than 17.1%. The bridge puts the total at 22%–28% of Georgian households, against the survey's 17.1%. The customs stock is still not used to size the Tbilisi market — the three readings in the next table all come from the survey itself, which is the more conservative choice and avoids compounding the bridge's own uncertainty into the scenarios.

Two operations on the water-heating tables that do not work

These tables invite two pieces of arithmetic that look reasonable and are not, and both circulate. Neither is used anywhere in this report.

  • 97.2% − 25.6% = 71.6% as "households with a water heater but no central heating". The subtraction appears nowhere in the publication, and it is a ceiling rather than a value — the two tables have no published cross-tabulation.
  • 97.2% × 87.8% read as a count of serviceable households. It double-counts, because a combi boiler is simultaneously the Table 3.11 central heating system and the Table 3.18 water heater.

The reconciliation above uses neither. It takes the gas water-heating population, which the publication does define, and splits it between boilers and kolonkas using the customs record — an external source the survey knows nothing about.

Why these three and not others

Every row is on the urban basis. Geostat's own table shows urban penetration running about 1.5× the national rate (25.6% against 17.1%), so a national rate applied to a city is not a like-for-like reading. Tbilisi is 96.8% urban, so the urban column is the right one throughout.

The customs stock is deliberately absent. It has no urban/rural split, so placing it here would require allocating national stock on an assumption, and the control test above shows the method missing by more than four times on one of the two products where its answer is known. It is reported nationally, where its limits are stated, and is not converted into a Tbilisi household count.

The verdict — how many boilers there are

This is a count of equipment, not of customers. Nothing in this table is an addressable market; who among them would ever pay for a service contract is Part 7's question.

How much the survival shape matters, and why an earlier figure was too low

A mean life of 7.4 years does not mean every boiler dies at 7.4. Some run to twelve or fifteen. An earlier version of this section used a hard cutoff at the mean, which allowed nothing to survive its own average life and understated the stock by about 18%.

With a realistic distribution the total is not very sensitive to the shape: 328,134 on a wide spread, 350,118 on the moderate one used here, and 358,268 on a tight one. The band is roughly 330,000 to 360,000 however the curve is drawn.

Three methods now agree. This gives Georgian household penetration of 23.2%–25.6%; the import bridge gave 22%–28%; and Geostat's own survey gives 17.1% as of May 2022, growing 8.4% a year on its own measurement. For Tbilisi this gives 34.7%–38.3% against the survey's trend reading of 35.4% and its water-heating reading of 38.5%. The question that opened Part 3 is closed.

The Tbilisi boiler base, stated

On a consistent urban basis the three readings span 105,241 to 158,273 Tbilisi households. The bottom of that range is the 2022 survey read literally and four years out of date; the top is its own water-heating tables cross-referenced against the customs record.

The published trend is what reconciles even that: the same Geostat table measured 17.1% in the 2017 round and 25.6% in the 2022 round — 8.4% growth a year. Carried forward four years to 2026 the survey's own number becomes 35.4%. The survey and the customs record are not really in conflict once the survey is read at the right date.

The honest summary for the meeting: “Geostat measured 25.6% of urban households in May 2022; its own published trend puts that near 35% today; and its water-heating tables, cross-referenced against the customs record, imply about 38%. That is a Tbilisi boiler base of roughly 105,000 to 158,000 households. The customs stock model suggests higher again, but it fails a control test and is not used to size Tbilisi.”

The 2024 comparator of 67,531 is in the 2024 Annual Report, Figure 3.12 ↗. Note the series is not monotonic — it fell from 69,235 in 2023 to 67,531 in 2024 before recovering.

Two figures that will be quoted at you, and what is wrong with both

“280,811 active enterprises.” Two thirds of them are individual entrepreneurs employing an average of 0.29 people — meaning most report nobody at all. They work from home, from a car, or from a rented stall. They do not have a boiler room.

“Non-household electricity consumers.” There are 152,808 of them at end-2025 (148,995 at end-2024). Electricity meters sit on kiosks, ATM cabinets, telecoms cabinets and lock-ups. The gas meter count — 72,068 nationally — is less than half of it, and only premises with a gas installation can have a gas boiler. Using the electricity figure overstates the universe by roughly 2.1×.

Growth tempo

The client asked specifically for this, and it is genuinely the strongest thing about the B2B case — and also the biggest hidden risk in it.

The survival rates are the finding, not the growth rate

A Tbilisi enterprise cohort loses more than half its members within three years and nearly seven in ten within five. Applied to a B2B subscription book that implies customer mortality of roughly 20–25% in the first year alone, before any competitive churn — the customer does not leave for a rival, it ceases to exist.

The plan under review assumes 2% monthly churn (21.5% a year) for its household book. That rate is well supported for households — it sits inside the HomeServe 79–87% and Frontdoor 79.2% retention band. It is not transferable to a Georgian B2B book, where insolvency alone matches it.

The business economy that sits behind those premises

Who you would be competing with

What is countable here, and what is not

The number of heating installers in Georgia is not published. Heating installation is NACE class 43.22, and Geostat publishes no enterprise count for it at three- or four-digit level. The Business Register goes only to Section (F Construction, 15,842 active); Business Demography goes to division (F43, 11,849). Anyone quoting a count of Georgian heating firms has constructed it.

F43 is specialised construction as a whole — electricians, plumbers, plasterers, painters, roofers, tilers, glaziers and demolition crews. Heating is a slice of one of its four groups.

What the division-level data does establish is the shape of the trade, and it is the part that matters commercially: 11,849 enterprises employing 12,585 people between them is 1.06 persons each, owner included. The typical competitor is one person with a van and no overhead. Entry is trivial and accelerating — F43 births ran 519 in 2017, 4,608 in 2023 and 3,267 in 2024.

That is the cost structure a subscription is priced against, and it is why the on-demand leg has a margin problem rather than a demand problem.

Institutional premises

One of these is shrinking, and it is worth knowing before it is quoted at you

Georgia's hospital count has fallen from 269 in 2022 to 232 in 2025, and hospital beds from 20,600 to 15,200, as COVID-era capacity was withdrawn. Tbilisi's public preschool count has been flat since 2019/2020 while enrolment fell 20%. The institutional premises stock is not a growth market.

A market size is only half the question. The other half is what a Tbilisi household can already buy, at what price, and from whom — because that is what a subscription has to beat. This part was researched directly in Georgian, from live product pages and classified listings read in August 2026, and it produced the sharpest findings in the study.

What a repair costs today

Verified against the live page

All twelve price strings in the first listing were searched for verbatim in the page served on 19 August 2026 and all twelve were found. The category page itself contains zero occurrences of წლიური, აბონემენტი, ყოველთვიური, გამოწერა or პაკეტი — no annual, subscription, monthly or package wording anywhere — which is the direct evidence behind the claim that no maintenance plan is advertised there.

Note the 70–200 GEL cleaning line: it is კალონკის გაწმენდა, cleaning a kolonka, not servicing a boiler. The "applies to" column marks which jobs are boiler work, which are kolonka work and which are general call-out charges.

These are also self-published tradesman prices on one marketplace, not an audited tariff. What they establish is the price a Tbilisi household is quoted, not the price the whole trade charges.

What this sets in the scenarios

These prices anchor the average job value used in Part 7: 70 / 110 / 150 GEL. A no-part repair is 50 GEL all-in, the most common paid part — a control board — starts at 130, and a realistic mix of adjustments, small parts and major parts lands in that band.

It is a low number, and it compounds the on-demand leg's difficulty: the work is cheap, the competition is one-person contractors with no overhead, and the gas distributor inspects for free. Part 7 therefore treats on-demand as market volume rather than as a profitable line.

The competitor that is free

Tbilisi Energy inspects the household gas installation for nothing, within 48 hours

The gas distributor runs a 24-hour hotline, sends a technician within 48 hours, checks the internal gas network and appliances, and issues a written safety act — completely free. It had inspected over 250,000 consumers by November 2023, finding violations in about 5%.

That kills one specific pitch: “we will come and check your system is safe” is already available at zero price from the company that sends the gas bill. What it does not do is service or repair the boiler, and it supplies no parts. The subscription has to be sold on maintenance and repair, never on safety inspection.

There is a second edge here worth having: Georgian Government Decree No. 101 of 22 January 2014 places responsibility for the safe operation of domestic gas appliances on the occupant. The liability a maintenance contract takes off the household's hands is already legally theirs, which is a real thing to sell against.

What the insurers do and do not cover

The deductible is the whole argument

Every Georgian home policy carries a deductible of 5% with a floor of GEL 100–150. A 50 GEL no-part repair and a 130 GEL control board are both below the deductible. Georgian home insurance cannot pay for a boiler repair even in principle, and it explicitly excludes wear and corrosion, which is what actually breaks boilers.

This cuts two ways and both should be said in the room. For: the need is real, no Georgian insurer this research checked is meeting it, and the gap looks structural rather than accidental. Against: four Georgian insurers were checked directly and none advertises the product; six more were searched without finding one. That is an absence of offers, not an observed refusal — but the underwriting logic points the same way, because wear on an appliance is a maintenance cost rather than an insurable fortuity. It is the reason to sell this as maintenance rather than as insurance.

Does anyone in Georgia pay for cover like this?

One question matters here: how many Georgians voluntarily hold and keep paying for cover they were not obliged to buy? Everything below is counted the same way — policies in force on a single date, against the population that could hold one.

How much of that 301,306 is households rather than companies

The regulator publishes the private/individual/state split for policies issued but not for policies in force, so it has to be imputed insurer by insurer from each one's issued mix:

  • About 246,500 in-force policies are held by individuals — 81.8% of the total, and 20.5% of Georgian households.
  • About 54,800 by companies and the state — 18.2% by count.

By count the book is retail; by money it is commercial. Companies hold 18% of the policies but account for 71.6% of property claims paid in 2025 — GEL 27.3m of GEL 38.1m — because a warehouse policy is worth many times a flat policy. For "how many Georgian households buy home cover", the count is the right measure.

The lower bound is 14.0%. The imputation assumes individuals and companies renew at the same rate within an insurer, and there is one place that is doubtful: TBC issued 569,139 policies, 98% of them to individuals, but kept only 14% to year end — a short cover attached to bank lending at a premium far below a real home policy. Aldagi's book behaves the opposite way, keeping 95%, and looks like genuine annual household insurance. If none of TBC's in-force policies were individual, the household figure falls to about 168,700, or 14.0%.

So the defensible range is 14.0% to 20.5% of Georgian households holding standing property cover — either way well above the 8.5% of vehicles carrying voluntary CASCO, and the strongest evidence in this report that Georgian households will pay for cover on their home when a channel they trust puts it in front of them.

A warning about the other number you will see quoted

The regulator also publishes policies issued during the year: 920,530 for property, and 15.5 million for life in a country of 3.9 million people. Those are counts of transactions — micro-covers stapled to bank products, most lasting weeks — not counts of insured homes or insured people. They are not a market size and should not be quoted as one. Every figure in the table above is an in-force count for exactly this reason.

This is the part of the report that decides whether a Tbilisi subscription target is ambitious or impossible, so it is built only from counts that an operator has filed and an auditor has seen. Market-research estimates of "market size in USD billions" are excluded deliberately: they are not measurements and they cannot be defended in a room.

The result of that discipline is itself the first finding. Exactly four countries publish an auditable count of households paying a recurring fee for home-systems maintenance or breakdown cover, and all four are high-income. Below Spain and Japan, nothing — not Poland, not Romania, not Türkiye, not Kazakhstan, not Georgia.

Four figures in the table above read wrong without these corrections

Every figure in it was re-checked against the filing it came from. The numbers all verified; four characterisations did not, and the corrected readings are these:

  • British Gas 12.2% is correct against UK households, but the product is sold in Great Britain only — against the market it actually serves it is 12.5%. The claim that it is “the highest measured anywhere in the world” is not sourced and is dropped; what stands is that it is the highest figure this research could find.
  • Frontdoor is the largest listed US home-warranty provider, not the whole sector. First American and Old Republic both write the line. Frontdoor alone is ~1.6% of occupied US housing units on a current denominator; the category is likely 3–4%.
  • HomeServe UK is ≈5.3%, and the source publishes “1.5m” to two significant figures, so the honest range is 5.1–5.5%.
  • US home warranty is not purely breakdown cover. Frontdoor separately sells maintenance services to warranty customers including pre-season HVAC tune-ups.

The Georgia reference row in the table uses 2014 census households. The correct current figures are 1,201,564 nationally and 411,098 in Tbilisi (2024 census, Part 1).

Read the Japan row before anything else in this report

HomeServe Japan is the closest published analogue to what is being proposed: a new entrant, in a market with no existing subscription culture, backed by utility partners who made 14 million households available for marketing under their own brands. In its third full financial year it had 41,000 customers — 0.29%.

The plan under review reaches 21,445 households in month twelve, in a city of 411,098 households, with no utility partner and no brand. That is a penetration of the whole city of 5.2% in year one, against 0.29% of a reachable base in year three for an operator with every structural advantage this one lacks.

What happened to the largest subscription book in the world

British Gas is the only operator anywhere that reports both sides of this question — a subscription book and an on-demand repair business — audited, for the same years. It is therefore the closest thing to a controlled test of the proposition in front of Casa Calda.

Three things this says about the proposition

  1. The book shrank. The deepest subscription business in the world lost 257,000 households in two years and fell from 13.3% to 12.2% of the country. Maturity is not a plateau; it is a peak followed by erosion, at 82% retention.
  2. On-demand is small beside a subscription book, not a feeder for it. 282,000 jobs against 3.4 million contracts is 8.2%. And Centrica states on page 7 that on-demand jobs fell "as we prioritised serving contract customers during periods of constrained capacity" — the two legs compete for the same engineers rather than one feeding the other. That is the single most transferable fact in this part, because the plan under review assumes on-demand acquires subscribers.
  3. The margin is thin and moving the wrong way. £388 revenue against £330 cost gave 15.0% in 2019; £361 against £338 gave 6.4% in 2021. A subscriber is worth roughly £23 a year of margin at an operator with a 200-year-old brand, its own billing relationship and 2.23 products per household.

What the product costs and what it pays out

The manufacturer-warranty argument — and why it cannot be used in Georgia yet

There is a well-known commercial argument for planned maintenance: the manufacturer makes an annual service a condition of the guarantee, and does not pay for it, so the household has to buy one anyway. In the United Kingdom that is verified and hard. Baxi condition 4 requires an annual service by a Gas Safe registered engineer and cuts the warranty to 12 months if it is missed; Vaillant clause 7 requires it and reserves the right to declare the guarantee null and void; Worcester Bosch makes it a condition and states non-compliance may reduce, limit or invalidate the guarantee. None of the three pays for the service.

None of that has been verified for Georgia, and it should not be asserted here. Every one of those documents is a UK terms-and-conditions page. Warranty terms in Georgia are set by the importer, not by the UK arm — Baxi is distributed here by Gorgia, Protherm by Vaillant's group under separate distribution, Bosch under different arrangements again. The Georgian warranty cards were not obtained, and the brands that actually dominate the Georgian market by volume are Turkish and Chinese (Part 3: Turkey 37.8%, China 31.4% of 2024 imports), not the UK-market brands these documents belong to.

What can be established from a Georgian source points the other way. Gorgia's own technical service-centre page offers free repair while the warranty card is valid („თუ თქვენს ნივთს ჯერ კიდევ აქვს მოქმედი გარანტია, მოგვმართეთ უფასო შეკეთებისთვის“), and states no annual-service condition at all. Nor does any Georgian importer publish a maintenance package.

Treat this as an open question with a cheap answer: obtain the warranty cards for the five best-selling boilers in Georgia and read the conditions. If they do impose a documented annual service, it is the strongest single argument for the subscription. If they do not — which the Gorgia page suggests — then the argument does not exist in this market and nothing in the pitch should rest on it.

Free warranty repair shrinks the paid repair market

If the importer repairs a boiler free while it is under warranty, then every boiler inside its guarantee period is outside the paid repair market. Part 1 shows the Tbilisi installed base skews young — 23.8% of dwellings built after 2000, and boilers arriving with new build and renovation — which means a larger share of the base than usual is still covered by someone else at zero price.

That is a reduction in the addressable on-demand market, not an increase, and the on-demand capture rates in Part 7 should be read with it in mind. It is also another reason the subscription has to be sold on planned maintenance and response time rather than on breakdown cover: breakdown on a young boiler is already free from the importer.

The legal question that must be settled before launch

A monthly fee that pays out on breakdown is a regulated product in most places that have looked at it. Florida defines a home warranty to include a contract where the provider “actually furnishes repair or replacement”, requires a licence, and makes unlicensed selling a first-degree misdemeanour. California issued cease-and-desist orders to four home-warranty sellers in September 2021 purely for lacking a licence.

For Georgia: the Law on Insurance contains no service-contract carve-out, Article 8³ requires Georgian citizens and companies to contract only with a licensed insurer, and Article 9³ bars use of the word დაზღვევა without a licence. Whether a repair-cover subscription is legally an insurance contract in Georgia is genuinely undetermined. This report does not answer it and nobody in the meeting should pretend to. It goes to Georgian counsel before a single policy is sold.

Everything in Parts 1 to 5 exists to make this part defensible. Each configuration is sized at three levels — minimum, middle and maximum — and the levels vary demand-side quantities only. Prices are held constant across all three, so the spread reads as "how many customers", not "how much did we assume we could charge".

These are steady-state market sizes, not year-one forecasts. They say how big each business can get, not how fast. The comparison against the plan is therefore generous to the plan: it puts a month-12 and a month-60 target against a ceiling that takes years to approach.

The two denominators every scenario divides into

Two different questions need two different bases, and conflating them is the commonest way a market size goes wrong.

  • How much breakdown work exists depends on how many boilers there are — every boiler in Tbilisi can fail, whoever owns it and whatever they are willing to pay.
  • How many customers can exist depends on how many buyers there are, and then on what share of them ever buys.

Nothing in this table is a count of people who want the product. Willingness is applied afterwards, as the penetration rate, and it is small — 2% to 12.2%.

What "able to pay" does and does not mean

It is a capability test, nothing more: can this household be charged every month. It needs one banked member, not every member banked. Findex measures adults — 64.5% of Georgian adults own a debit card, 78.8% hold an account — and converting those to a household rate on the census composition gives 85–88% by card and 94–95% by account. A standing order runs off an account, not only a card. Geostat's own household-level measure, 96.9% of Tbilisi households with internet access, points the same way.

It is not a measure of demand. Of the households that could pay, the model assumes between 2% and 12.2% ever do — and the top of that range is what British Gas reached after thirty years. A universe of {u['mid']['total_boilers_in_scope']:,} boilers is not {u['mid']['total_boilers_in_scope']:,} customers; at the middle case the subscription-only B2C configuration produces {S[('sub_b2c','mid')]['distinct_customers']:,}.

Why the commercial penetration is set higher than the household one

B2C runs 2% / 6.3% / 12.2%; B2B runs 3% / 8% / 15%. A commercial occupier buys maintenance as an operating expense, has a budget line for it, and loses money the day the heat fails — a cold office or a hotel without hot water has a cost that a family postponing a repair does not. One signature also covers several boilers.

The offset is not appetite, it is mortality. Tbilisi enterprise survival is 46.0% at three years and 30.7% at five, so a B2B book loses roughly a fifth of itself a year to insolvency before any competitive churn. That is carried separately, in the retention parameter, not by suppressing the share who buy.

Seven configurations, three levels each

These are ceilings, not years. Each figure is the level at which the business stops growing because new customers only replace the ones it loses — steady state. No date attaches to any of them, because nothing in this model says how fast a Georgian operator could climb.

What the evidence does say is roughly where on a curve each level sits. HomeServe Japan reached 0.29% of its reachable households in year three, with utility partners handing over 14 million households. HomeServe's affinity markets settle at 6.3–6.6% after decades. British Gas reached 12.2% of UK households over roughly thirty years, and has since been losing it. So the three levels below are better read as three points on a long curve than as three views of the same year.

Two columns that decide whether the book can be held

Gross adds a year just to stand still. A steady-state book is not a stock you reach and keep. At 78% retention, the 8,435-customer B2C book loses 1,856 households a year and has to replace every one of them before it grows by a single customer. That is what the sales cost buys, and it never stops.

Engineers at winter peak. Georgian households heat for a mean of 5.7 months. Roughly two thirds of the year's field events fall inside that window, so the annual-mean engineer count understates what has to be on the payroll in November by about 60%. A heating business staffed to its annual mean fails in the only months that matter.

The comparison this whole report exists to make

The largest market any combination of assumptions in this research produces is 30,469 distinct customers and 19,046,683 GEL a year — the subscription + on-demand + insurance configuration with every demand parameter at its optimistic end. The boiler base is the most generous of the three survey readings — Table 3.19's urban gas share crossed with the 2024 import split, 38.5% — not the customs stock model, which has no urban split and is reported nationally only. The subscription penetration is the highest figure this research could find anywhere.

The plan under review reaches 21,445 customers in month twelve — 70% of that ceiling, in one year — and 97,760 in month sixty, which is 321% of it.

Nothing in this report closes that gap. It is not a matter of the boiler base being larger than Geostat says — Part 3 grants that it probably is, and the maximum case is built on the customs reading, not the survey one. The gap survives every generous assumption available.

Every parameter, and what it is anchored to

What this model deliberately does not do

It sizes a market. It is not a P&L, and five things that decide whether the business makes money are outside it on purpose, because each needs a measurement nobody has taken yet:

  • Margin. Revenue and headcount only. The separate 60-month P&L model in this engagement concludes the on-demand leg never breaks even at any horizon tested to month 240 — so read the on-demand rows here as market volume, not as profit.
  • Time. Every figure is steady state. Nothing here says how long it takes, and the honest comparator is HomeServe Japan: 0.29% of reachable households in year three, with a utility handing over 14 million households.
  • Customer acquisition cost. Absent. It is computable from the client's own call log — 2,340 dials produced 442 interested and 115 who named 50 GEL or more — and it is the single parameter most likely to decide the answer.
  • VAT. Registration is mandatory above 100,000 GEL of turnover, which every scenario except the minima crosses immediately. Whether 50 GEL is a VAT-inclusive consumer price is an open question for the plan's author and is worth roughly 18/118 of the subscription line.
  • Parts. The model does not say whether parts are inside the subscription or billed on top. British Gas solves this with a £60 excess per claim; this model does not solve it at all.

What would move these numbers most

  1. Casa Calda's own service history. Call volume, job value, repeat rate and seasonality from the existing service entity would replace four judgement parameters with measurements. It costs nothing and it is still the highest-value action available.
  2. A paid smoke test of the 50 GEL tier. Real landing page, real checkout, three to six months. Stated willingness to pay overstates conversion by two to three times, and no amount of desk research substitutes for a measured conversion rate.
  3. The legal question. If a breakdown rider requires an insurance licence in Georgia, the insurance column of this table becomes a partnership negotiation rather than a product decision.

Where every cost comes from

The cost side of this model is not independent research. It is the client's own business plan — their line items, their salary figures — with nine changes, each listed below with the reason. Where their number is defensible it is used unchanged.

Two structural differences go beyond the individual corrections. Their overhead is flat: 47,060 GEL of payroll and 26,550 of marketing, identical in month 1 and month 60, whether the business has 700 customers or 97,760. Here it grows with the business and reaches their own figures at their own scale — the top tier is their 42,500 exactly. And they budget marketing but employ nobody to convert it, while an earlier version of this model employed sellers but bought no demand. Both are carried here.

Why the on-demand leg loses money

It is not the overhead. It is the price of the work.

Putting one engineer in front of one customer costs this business 68 GEL — the client's own crew salary of 4,560 plus a real service vehicle at 1,200, over 4.5 jobs a day, 22 working days, at 85% utilisation. That is the cost of turning up, before parts, before overhead, before anyone answers the phone.

Now set it against what Tbilisi tradesmen actually charge.

Only 3 of the 9 published job types clear the cost of the visit

Everything at or below roughly 78 GEL loses money outright. That includes the two most common calls in the trade: the diagnostic visit and the adjustment that needs no part.

The reason is structural, not managerial. The 50 GEL no-part repair is priced by a one-man operator whose cost is his own time — no crew, no supervisor, no dispatcher, no vehicle fleet, no marketing. Part 4 measures that trade: NACE division F43 holds 11,849 active enterprises employing 12,585 people between them, an average of 1.06 each. A company cannot deliver at the price an individual charges, and in Tbilisi the individual sets the price.

The three jobs that do clear — system clean, control board, fan — average 167 GEL and carry about 45 GEL of margin each. A company could build a business on those alone. But it cannot advertise for them: the customer does not know which they need until an engineer has already driven out and diagnosed it, at a cost of 68 GEL.

Which is why on-demand belongs inside a subscription, not beside it

A subscriber's boiler is already visited twice a year on a planned schedule. The engineer is already there, the drive is already paid for, and the diagnosis is already done. The cost of turning up has been absorbed by the subscription fee, so the parts job that follows carries its full margin rather than paying for the visit twice.

That is the whole argument for the combined configurations, and it is why every one of them clears break-even in the tables below while both standalone legs do not.

The first year, and the 700 the plan begins with

Two numbers matter more than anything else in a first-year discussion: how many customers a real sales effort actually lands, and where the plan's opening balance of 700 came from.

The realistic first year is about 1,000 subscribers, not 21,445

In the central case the subscription-only business ends year one with 973 customers, and with an on-demand leg attached, 2,259. The plan under review ends year one with 21,445.

The gap is not a disagreement about demand. It is that the plan has no acquisition mechanism at all: its customer count is 700 compounded at 35% a month for twelve months, and nothing in the workbook connects that growth to a salesperson, a campaign or a cost. Its marketing budget is a flat 26,550 GEL a month that does not vary with how many customers it is supposed to be producing.

Where does the 700 come from? — the question to put to the author first

Nothing in the workbook derives it. It is Assumptions!B11, a typed constant, with no formula, no source note and no supporting calculation on any of the eight sheets. It feeds Revenue Model C5 as "Beginning Customers" in month 1, is immediately grown by 35%, and ends month one at 931.

Every figure in the plan descends from it. 700 × 1.33 compounded twelve times is the 21,445 of year one; carried on to month 60 it is the 97,760 that the whole 1.35M GEL request rests on. Change 700 and every number in the plan changes proportionally.

On this model, reaching 700 subscribers from a standing start takes month 8 in the central case, or month 6 with an on-demand leg feeding attach. At the pessimistic level the subscription-only business does not reach 700 within five years at all. The plan treats as its opening balance a position that costs six to eight months of full sales effort to reach.

Three things to ask, in this order

  1. Are these existing Casa Calda customers? If the 700 are warm relationships from the existing MEP business — installations under warranty, past clients, building contracts — then the number is not an assumption at all, it is an asset, and it is the most valuable thing in the plan. It would also change the acquisition arithmetic completely: converting a past customer costs a fraction of a cold dial, and Part 6 shows that affinity conversion is the only channel that reaches 6% anywhere in the world.
  2. If they are, how many are there and what is the evidence they convert? A list of 700 names is not 700 subscribers. The client's own survey converted 2,340 dials into 115 people who named 50 GEL or more — 4.9%. If the 700 are warm, what rate should replace that?
  3. If they are not existing customers, what acquires them, and at what cost? The plan carries no CAC — Assumptions!B31 is blank, which is why its own LTV/CAC cell renders #DIV/0! while the dashboard asserts a ratio above 30×.

This is the single highest-value question in the review. It costs one conversation and it either validates the plan's starting point or removes it.

What this table says

  • Neither on-demand nor insurance works on its own, at any level. On-demand comes closest at the optimistic end and still returns −3% in year five. The reason is above: only three of nine published Tbilisi job types clear the cost of sending an engineer, and the two commonest calls in the trade lose money before a single overhead is charged.
  • The subscription is what makes the rest work. Every configuration that pairs a subscription with something else clears break-even by year two in the central case. The subscription pays for the visit; the parts job that follows carries its full margin instead of paying for the drive twice.
  • B2B is the strongest single line by margin — 24% at the middle level against 9% for B2C, and 42% at the optimistic end — because one contract covers several boilers and four planned visits a year is cheap to deliver against a 120 GEL fee. What a subscriber costs to win — the model produces it, nobody has to assume it

Cost to acquire a subscriber is not an input here. It falls out of the sales arithmetic — agents, dials, close rate, a list that gets used up — and it is the most uncomfortable line in these tables.

In the central case, fully loaded with the marketing behind it, it runs 244 GEL in year one, 394 in year two, 566 in year three and 774 in year four. A subscriber is worth about 2,012 GEL of lifetime gross margin at 78% retention, so the ratio falls from 8.2× to 2.6× across four years.

The cause is list exhaustion, not incompetence. Six agents dialling 1,100 numbers a month work through a list of 134,000 addressable households in under two years. A household called a second time converts worse than one called for the first time, so the same spend buys fewer customers every year.
